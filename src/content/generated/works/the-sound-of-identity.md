## Abstract

Music is both a record of social life and a resource through which people organize identity, belonging, memory, distinction, and collective action. Yet public discussion often reduces this relationship to stereotypes: hip-hop is assigned to criminality, rock to rebellion, pop to passive consumption, and metal to aggression. This structured integrative review develops a more defensible account of genre cultures by synthesizing research from popular-music studies, cultural geography, developmental psychology, communication, public health, and peace and conflict studies. It asks how music participates in cultural and geographical identity; how rock, metal, hip-hop, pop, folk/fusion, and electronic music organize contrasting but overlapping social worlds; how age and generation shape musical identification; how peer relations and substance use should be interpreted without causal overstatement; and under what conditions music can express grievance, mobilize protest, normalize violent identities, or support peacebuilding. Bangladesh is used as a central case through scholarship on linguistic nationalism, Liberation War broadcasting, Rabindrasangīt, folk-to-pop continuity, Dhaka’s metal scene, hip-hop activism, and transnational K-pop fandom. The synthesis argues that genre is neither a direct cause of conduct nor a neutral label. Genres operate as flexible cultural repertoires whose meanings are produced through scenes, media, language, class, gender, geography, political history, and commercial institutions. In conflict, music can compress political narratives, synchronize emotion, mark allies and enemies, carry propaganda, preserve testimony, and enable mourning. These capacities can serve protest, war-making, reconciliation, or several at once. Music rarely creates collective violence independently; its effects depend on prior grievances, organized actors, media access, coercive institutions, and opportunities for action. Bangladesh challenges universal models derived from Western settings because global forms are repeatedly localized through Bangla language, liberation history, religious and secular contestation, class resources, and digital circulation. The article concludes with an agenda for a multisite, longitudinal mixed-method study capable of distinguishing musical selection from peer socialization and expression from behavioral effect while preserving the interpretive complexity of genre cultures.

**Keywords:** music and identity; genre cultures; Bangladesh; rock; hip-hop; popular music; protest music; political violence; peacebuilding; youth culture; peer groups; substance use

## 1. Introduction

Music accompanies collective ritual, private emotion, political mobilization, fashion, friendship, memory, and everyday routine. Its social force does not arise only from lyrics or sound. It also emerges from the places where music is performed, the people with whom it is heard, the media through which it circulates, and the identities attached to artists and genres. A concert, streaming playlist, fan group, rehearsal room, political rally, family gathering, and national commemoration can make the same recording mean different things. Music therefore functions both as a reflection of culture and as material used to produce culture.

Popular explanations often convert this reciprocal relationship into deterministic caricature. Hip-hop becomes “gangster music,” rock becomes a lifestyle of alienation or rebellion, pop becomes commercial conformity, and metal becomes aggression. Such descriptions may identify images circulating within particular scenes, but they fail as general accounts. Gangsta rap is one branch within a heterogeneous hip-hop history that also includes party music, political testimony, religious expression, social commentary, experimental art, and commercial entertainment. Rock contains grunge, progressive rock, punk, alternative rock, arena rock, and numerous local hybrids whose participants disagree about authenticity and success. Pop fandom can be commercially organized while also sustaining intense creativity, community, political action, and cross-cultural learning. Genre categories themselves vary by country, period, industry, and platform.

The more useful question is not whether a genre possesses a single lifestyle. It is how genres offer symbolic repertoires through which artists and listeners negotiate authenticity, morality, status, masculinity and femininity, resistance, aspiration, community, and the meaning of a worthwhile life. Nirvana and Pearl Jam can be read within grunge and alternative-rock formations that made alienation, vulnerability, distrust of celebrity, and arguments over commercial authenticity publicly available. Audioslave joined alternative-metal sonics to political and introspective themes. Dream Theater made technical mastery, complexity, and dedicated listening central to progressive-metal identity. Nile provides an example of technical death metal organized around extremity, musicianship, and historical or mythological imagery. These artists are useful cases, but no band can stand for all listeners, and no listener acquires an ideology simply by pressing play.

Bangladesh is especially productive for examining these dynamics. Its musical field connects language, nationalism, liberation memory, folk and devotional traditions, secular and religious contestation, postcolonial modernity, urban middle-class scenes, global rock and metal, hip-hop activism, commercial pop, and digitally organized transnational fandom. Research on Dhaka’s metal scene shows how a global genre became a locally specific alternative space shaped by political frustration and unequal access to cultural resources (Quader & Redden, 2015; Quader, 2016). Work on Rabindrasangīt and Language Movement songs demonstrates that music has also participated in the formation and contestation of national identity (Bajpaie, 2025; Ila, 2017). Research on K-pop listeners shows another configuration in which online networks support cross-cultural identities among Bangladeshi young adults (Sultana & Nibir, 2024).

This review addresses six questions:

1. Through what social and spatial mechanisms does music reflect and produce cultural and geographical identity?
2. How do rock, metal, hip-hop, pop, folk/fusion, and electronic music organize different but overlapping genre cultures?
3. How do age, generation, memory, peer networks, and media environments shape musical identification?
4. What does existing evidence support about relationships among music, peer groups, and substance use?
5. How does Bangladesh extend or challenge theories developed mainly in Western settings?
6. Under what conditions does music express and mobilize protest, legitimate or intensify violence, or support memory, healing, and reconciliation?

The central argument is that music shapes culture through a reciprocal process of **selection, identification, performance, and reinforcement**. People select music partly because it resonates with existing dispositions and circumstances. Music then provides names, styles, narratives, affects, and communities through which those dispositions can be expressed and reorganized. Peers, scenes, platforms, and institutions reinforce or contest these meanings. The result is neither direct media control nor complete listener autonomy.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Review design

This article is a structured integrative review. It combines empirical and theoretical research from cultural sociology, popular-music studies, ethnomusicology, cultural geography, developmental psychology, communication, and public health. An integrative design was selected because the questions span interpretive ethnography, historical analysis, surveys, longitudinal friendship studies, network research, and media-content analysis. These designs do not estimate a common effect and should not be pooled as if they were interchangeable.

The review is not presented as a PRISMA systematic review or meta-analysis. It was not preregistered, did not use independent dual screening, and did not search every subscription database. Its purpose is theory-building and comparative synthesis rather than an exhaustive effect estimate.

### 2.2 Search strategy

Searches were conducted on 11 October 2026 through web-indexed academic discovery, DOI records, publisher pages, PubMed and PubMed Central, institutional repositories, journal sites, and the author’s existing research repositories. Search strings combined variants of *music*, *identity*, *place*, *geography*, *scene*, *subculture*, *rock*, *metal*, *hip-hop*, *pop*, *adolescence*, *age*, *peer*, *substance use*, *Bangladesh*, *Bangla rock*, *Language Movement*, *nationalism*, *protest music*, *political mobilization*, *war songs*, *propaganda*, *incitement*, *hate music*, *violence*, *conflict transformation*, and *peacebuilding*.

Eligible records had to establish a relevant mechanism, report empirical evidence, provide a comparative case, or directly address Bangladesh. Peer-reviewed articles and scholarly books were prioritized. Journalism, community forums, tertiary summaries, and unverified AI-generated citations were excluded from the scholarly evidence base. The author’s repositories, `bangladesh-music-evolution` and `listener-taste-growth-profile`, were used to discover sources and prior conceptual work. Their AI-assisted notes were treated as leads, not evidence. Sources derived from them were included only after independent bibliographic verification.

More than 35 potentially relevant records were examined during the initial stage, with a core corpus retained for synthesis. The review also records negative findings cautiously. For example, the search found substantially richer scholarship on Bangladeshi rock and metal than on Bangladeshi mainstream pop, but this does not prove that no additional pop scholarship exists in Bangla-language, unindexed, or inaccessible sources.

### 2.3 Analytical approach

Sources were read across ten dimensions: identity scale, geography, genre, age or life stage, social mechanism, conflict or peacebuilding function, method, main finding, causal strength, and transferability to Bangladesh. Evidence was evaluated according to disciplinary purpose. Ethnography was assessed for interpretive depth, reflexivity, sampling, and contextual specificity. Behavioral studies were assessed for temporality, measurement, confounding, mediation logic, and network structure. Content analyses were treated as evidence about representations and potential exposure, not behavioral effects. Studies of propaganda, incitement, protest, and peacebuilding were additionally assessed for the political organization, media system, coercive setting, and audience practices through which music could acquire force.

The synthesis uses an abductive strategy. It begins with established concepts such as social identity, music scenes, cultural capital, glocalization, selection, socialization, and reinforcing spirals, then evaluates how well those concepts explain the Bangladesh material. Contradictions are retained rather than forced into a single model.

## 3. Music as Identity Work

Music helps people narrate who they are, who they were, and with whom they belong. In adolescence, it can support enjoyment, mood regulation, social image, friendship, possible selves, and differentiation from adults or rival peer groups (Miranda, 2013). These uses make taste socially legible. Preferences can operate as badges that communicate values, dispositions, and desired affiliations, even when the inference drawn by others is inaccurate.

The badge metaphor should not imply a stable uniform. A listener may use death metal for technical admiration, pop for dancing, folk music for family connection, and film music for nostalgia. Private taste may diverge from the music displayed to peers. People also move between genre cultures across settings and life stages. Musical identity is therefore better modeled as a repertoire than a permanent category.

Friendship research supports the social-marker function. In a two-wave Dutch study, similarity in musical preference was associated with forming new friendships, although it did not predict the termination of existing friendships (Selfhout et al., 2009). A later longitudinal network analysis also examined how musical preference, friendship, and externalizing behavior coevolve in early adolescence (Franken et al., 2017). These studies make selection central: listeners do not enter peer groups randomly, and apparent influence may partly reflect choosing similar others.

Identity work also includes classification and hierarchy. Listeners learn which artists count as authentic, commercial, technically accomplished, emotionally honest, politically serious, fashionable, or outdated. These judgments can provide cultural capital inside a scene. Quader’s (2016) analysis of Dhaka metal shows that participation required more than preference. Instruments, rehearsal time, English-language access, travel, media exposure, studios, venues, and relationships with influential scene members distributed the capacity to become a recognized participant. Taste becomes socially consequential when institutions and resources allow it to be performed.

## 4. Musical Geography: Place, Scene, and Glocalization

Music is geographically situated even when it travels globally. Scene theory distinguishes local, translocal, and virtual forms of collective musical life (Bennett & Peterson, 2004). A local scene depends on recurring interaction among performers, audiences, venues, promoters, media, and institutions. A translocal scene links comparable communities across cities or nations. A virtual scene sustains interaction through communication networks that may have no single physical center.

This framework avoids two errors. The first treats geography as a container in which a ready-made genre happens. The second assumes that digital circulation has made place irrelevant. Places are actively produced through sound, memory, infrastructure, language, and stories about origin. Bennett and Rogers (2016) show that scenes persist through cultural memory and emotional geography after their most active periods. A closed venue, a remembered concert, or an earlier generation’s repertoire can remain part of a city’s identity.

Comparative research demonstrates that globalization and localization are intertwined. Hong Kong popular music developed through the mediation of international industries, local production, social circumstances, and audience identification (Ho, 2003). Singaporean popular music likewise remained expressive of local and national influences within transnational circulation (Kong, 1997). Research on diaspora further identifies music as a resource for recognition, resistance, memory, identity maintenance, and identity transformation across places (Lidskog, 2017). Geographic analysis of music preferences in the United States found patterned associations among metropolitan taste, class, density, diversity, and political-economic divisions, although the study relied on a self-selected internet sample and aggregate associations (Mellander et al., 2018).

The relevant process is glocalization: global musical forms are translated through local language, histories, institutions, and inequalities. A distorted guitar does not carry one universal politics. Its meaning changes when joined to Bangla lyrics, performed at a Dhaka venue, circulated through local media, or placed within the memory of the Liberation War. Similarly, hip-hop’s global conventions of flow, sampling, locality, style, and credibility can be used to articulate Bangladesh-specific grievances rather than reproduce an American social world.

## 5. Comparative Genre Cultures

### 5.1 From genre labels to cultural repertoires

Genre is simultaneously sonic classification, market category, historical tradition, and social claim. Treating genre cultures as repertoires allows comparison without assuming that every member holds the same worldview. The relevant dimensions include:

- ideas of authenticity and commercial success;
- preferred forms of participation, such as attentive listening, dance, live performance, online fandom, or lyrical interpretation;
- relationships to place, language, class, gender, ethnicity, religion, and generation;
- characteristic narratives about resistance, aspiration, pleasure, virtuosity, vulnerability, and community;
- institutional infrastructures such as venues, labels, festivals, media, platforms, and fan networks.

These dimensions are tendencies to investigate, not definitions imposed in advance.

### 5.2 Hip-hop and rap: locality, testimony, enterprise, and contested respectability

Hip-hop is frequently racialized and criminalized in public discourse. The statement that hip-hop “belongs to gangsters” mistakes one subgenre, one marketing history, and one representational vocabulary for the whole cultural field. Gangsta rap made street violence, policing, illicit economies, masculinity, and territorial credibility prominent themes in particular American contexts. Those representations matter, but hip-hop also contains political critique, religious expression, comedy, romance, introspection, community education, experimental practice, and commercially oriented entertainment.

The concept of credibility is more useful than the label “gangster.” Hip-hop artists and audiences may evaluate whether a voice is truthful to lived experience, loyal to a locality, technically skilled, politically courageous, commercially compromised, or entrepreneurially successful. These standards can produce opposing moral judgments within the same genre. Material success may be celebrated as survival or condemned as inauthentic, depending on scene and speaker.

Bangladeshi hip-hop makes the need for localization visible. Hasan and Kundu (2022) place hip-hop activism within a longer national tradition of protest music, while contemporary work on the July 2024 uprising has been identified in the author’s prior research as an important emerging source requiring full integration in a later update. Bangla rap may borrow a global form while locating credibility in local language, political frustration, urban experience, and generational conflict. It should not be analyzed as an imitation whose meaning is exhausted by American gangsta imagery.

### 5.3 Grunge and alternative rock: alienation, vulnerability, and authenticity

Grunge and alternative rock provide a contrasting repertoire. Bands such as Nirvana and Pearl Jam became associated with alienation, emotional unease, skepticism toward celebrity, and disputes about commercial authenticity. Their listeners have sometimes used these themes to articulate distance from polished mainstream culture. Yet grunge was also produced and distributed by major commercial institutions, and its authenticity was continually negotiated rather than naturally possessed.

The philosophical appeal of this repertoire can include suspicion of status competition, solidarity with outsiders, emotional candor, and criticism of social expectations. It would be inaccurate, however, to assign these beliefs to all listeners. Some may value guitar tone, personal nostalgia, a specific performer, or the social memory of the 1990s without adopting a coherent ideology.

Pearl Jam and Nirvana are especially useful as a comparison because they occupied a shared historical formation while presenting different public relationships to longevity, celebrity, activism, and performance. Their contrast shows why genre membership does not determine one lifestyle. The scene provides arguments and symbols that artists and fans use differently.

### 5.4 Alternative metal and political-introspective rock

Audioslave demonstrates how genre lineages can be recombined. Its members connected alternative rock, metal, and the political associations carried by earlier projects. For listeners, such music may support identities organized around intensity, political skepticism, self-examination, or the perceived sincerity of live musicianship. Again, this is an available interpretive repertoire, not a behavioral diagnosis.

The wider category also reveals the limits of a rock-versus-pop binary. Alternative metal can use mass media, major labels, celebrity, and arena performance while retaining an oppositional aesthetic. Commercial circulation does not automatically erase political meaning, just as an underground location does not guarantee political radicalism.

### 5.5 Progressive rock and metal: complexity, expertise, and devoted listening

Progressive rock and metal often place unusual value on technical competence, compositional complexity, extended form, instrumental knowledge, and repeated attentive listening. Dream Theater is a useful case because virtuosity and formal complexity are central to its reception. Participation may involve learning equipment, debating performance technique, comparing compositions, and accumulating specialized knowledge. These practices can become cultural capital.

The lifestyle associated with progressive music is therefore less adequately described through rebellion than through disciplined fandom and expertise. Listeners may value mastery, patience, precision, intellectual challenge, or escape into elaborate musical worlds. These values can overlap with professional, educational, or hobbyist identities. They can also generate hierarchy, gatekeeping, and exclusion when technical knowledge becomes a test of legitimate belonging.

### 5.6 Death metal and extreme music: symbolic extremity without behavioral equivalence

Extreme metal uses sonic density, speed, distortion, harsh vocals, violent imagery, death, religion, mythology, and horror. Nile, for example, is a productive case for examining how technical death metal joins virtuosity with ancient-Egyptian imagery. The important analytical distinction is between symbolic extremity and actual conduct. A listener’s attraction to difficult sound, transgressive imagery, or fictional violence does not establish violent behavior or a single political philosophy.

Extreme genres may provide controlled spaces for confronting fear, mortality, taboo, anger, or social estrangement. They can also organize highly knowledgeable communities around performance technique and genre history. Moral-panic interpretations overlook the difference between aesthetic participation and literal endorsement.

### 5.7 Pop: accessibility, performance, fandom, and transnational intimacy

Pop is often positioned as rock’s commercial other. This contrast overstates rock’s independence from industry and understates the agency of pop audiences. Pop cultures may emphasize hooks, visual performance, dance, fashion, celebrity narratives, accessibility, and rapid media circulation. These features can support broad participation and transnational fandom rather than merely passive consumption.

Sultana and Nibir’s (2024) study of K-pop among Bangladeshi young adults connects production quality, lyrics, choreography, performers, fashion, and online networks to a cross-cultural sense of self. K-pop fandom demonstrates that commercial organization can coexist with active interpretation, fan labor, social belonging, and cultural learning. The relationship is not politically innocent, since industries cultivate intimacy and consumption, but neither is it socially empty.

Pop also requires more direct study in Bangladesh. The current literature gives metal, rock, folk fusion, heritage music, and political repertoires richer analytical treatment than mainstream Bangladeshi pop. This imbalance can cause scholars to mistake what is well studied for what is culturally most important.

### 5.8 Folk, fusion, and heritage music: continuity, land, spirituality, and nation

Folk and heritage repertoires are central to cultural memory in Bangladesh. Baul, Bhatiali, Bhawaiya, Rabindrasangīt, Nazrul music, Language Movement songs, and Liberation War repertoires connect sound to land, language, spirituality, history, and national imagination. Mridha and Begum (2023) describe a continuum from folk to modern and pop music, while emphasizing the persistence of local themes and the blending of local and foreign forms.

Heritage should not be treated as an uncontested past. Bajpaie (2025) shows that Rabindrasangīt has participated in anti-sectarian and nationalist projects while remaining politically contested in Bangladesh. Ila’s (2017) analysis of Language Movement songs places musical form inside the emotional and historical development of Bengali nationalism. Music does not simply preserve national identity; it helps define which version of the nation is remembered and authorized.

Fusion makes continuity dynamic rather than pure. Global instrumentation or production does not necessarily displace local identity. It may provide a new means of activating folk texts, melodic traditions, or philosophical themes for different audiences. The analytical question is who controls the translation and what forms of cultural authority it produces.

### 5.9 Electronic and dance cultures: body, nightlife, technology, and temporary community

Electronic and dance music foreground bodily synchronization, repetition, technology, DJs, nightlife, and festival space. Their communities can be intense yet temporary, organized less by a stable ideological program than by recurring events and affective participation. This helps explain why scene theory may be more appropriate than a bounded subculture model.

Dance-music preference has been associated with substance use in some adolescent studies, but the association can reflect nightlife access, peer selection, sensation seeking, and local club culture rather than properties of sound. Genre labels such as dance, techno, or hardhouse also change meaning across countries and periods. Behavioral inference therefore requires measures of setting and peer practice, not genre preference alone.

## 6. Bangladesh: Layered Sonic Identities

### 6.1 Language, liberation, and competing national imaginaries

Bangladesh’s history makes sound inseparable from linguistic and political identity. Language Movement songs linked Bangla to collective emotion and nationalist development (Ila, 2017). Rabindrasangīt contributed to a syncretic Bengali subjectivity and was mobilized across anti-sectarian, linguistic-nationalist, and liberation contexts, even as its place in national culture remained contested (Bajpaie, 2025).

This history complicates any clean separation between culture and politics. A song can be artistic heritage, personal memory, national symbol, secular statement, religious controversy, and political resource at the same time. It can also change meaning as governments, institutions, and movements recontextualize it.

### 6.2 The Dhaka metal scene

Quader and Redden’s (2015) study remains the strongest direct empirical account of a Bangladeshi contemporary genre scene. Drawing on 14 interviews, participant observation at underground and mainstream gigs, and textual materials, the authors describe a predominantly male, middle-class, part-time scene in which global metal forms were used to create an alternative cultural space. Participants expressed frustration with national conditions and distinguished the underground from mainstream rock and pop, but many did not treat commercial success as an ethical betrayal.

This finding matters because it challenges a Western-derived story in which underground authenticity necessarily requires opposition to commercial success. In Dhaka, the underground could operate as an experimental incubator and pathway toward recognition. Scene boundaries were real but permeable.

Quader (2016) further demonstrates that scene participation depended on economic, cultural, and social capital. Equipment, lessons, rehearsal time, studios, English-language education, travel, foreign-media access, and recognition from senior figures helped determine who could participate. A musical lifestyle is therefore partly a livelihood question. Musicians need resources, work arrangements, and networks that allow artistic labor, even when the scene itself generates little income.

This is an important distinction between **lifestyle** and **livelihood**. Lifestyle refers to performed identity, dress, leisure, values, and affiliation. Livelihood concerns income, time, equipment, work, and material survival. Romantic accounts of rock rebellion often celebrate lifestyle while ignoring who can afford to maintain it.

### 6.3 Bangla rock, fusion, and postcolonial localization

Mukherjee (2017) interprets Bangla rock through counterculture and dissidence in the broader Bengali cultural field. Dorin (2013) traces the political and social dimensions of Bengali popular music through protest-song traditions and later rock formations in Calcutta. These works are not direct substitutes for Bangladesh research, but they illuminate a connected linguistic region in which global rock conventions were vernacularized.

The key process is not Westernization followed by local imitation. Bangla lyrics, folk materials, political history, urban experience, and local performance institutions transform the genre. The result may remain recognizably rock while carrying meanings that cannot be inferred from British or American histories alone.

### 6.4 Rap, pop, and digital transnationalism

Hip-hop and K-pop show two different forms of contemporary transnationalism. Bangladeshi rap localizes a global form through vernacular expression, political grievance, and urban credibility. K-pop fandom connects listeners outward through highly organized digital and commercial networks (Sultana & Nibir, 2024). One form may emphasize local testimony while another foregrounds cross-cultural aspiration, yet both depend on platforms and peer networks.

This comparison also shows why “rock versus pop” is insufficient. Bangladesh’s current musical ecology includes overlapping repertoires, hybrid playlists, online fandoms, heritage revival, protest music, film music, metal scenes, and commercial production. Listeners can occupy several of these worlds at once.

## 7. Age, Generation, and Cultural Memory

### 7.1 Adolescence and musical intensity

Adolescence is a period of identity exploration, peer reorientation, emotional change, and growing control over media choice. Music can become unusually important because it supports all four processes at once (Miranda, 2013). This developmental intensity helps explain why later memory often privileges music encountered during youth.

Large cross-sectional studies show patterned age differences in engagement and preference. Bonneville-Roussy et al. (2013) found that the importance and frequency of music listening declined with age, although music remained important in adulthood. Younger participants listened in more varied social contexts, while adults listened more privately. Preferences for intense and contemporary music tended to decline across age groups, while unpretentious and sophisticated preferences increased. A later Music Preferences in Adulthood Model separated individual differences, social influences, and perceived musical attributes as determinants of age trends (Bonneville-Roussy et al., 2017).

These findings should not be converted into a natural law that people inevitably abandon rock, metal, rap, or pop. The studies are largely cross-sectional. Age, cohort, and historical period remain entangled. A 50-year-old’s preferences may reflect aging, the repertoire available during adolescence, changing family and work contexts, platform adoption, or generational identity.

### 7.2 Cohort, period, and life course

A cohort experiences particular technologies, political events, media institutions, and musical repertoires during formative years. A period effect influences multiple ages at once, such as the arrival of streaming or a political uprising. A life-course effect follows changing roles, time, resources, or emotional goals. Strong generational analysis must distinguish all three.

Bangladesh offers several plausible periodizing contexts: the Language Movement, the Liberation War, post-independence band music, the growth of cassette and CD markets, the rock and metal scenes of the 1990s and 2000s, satellite television, MP3 circulation, social media, streaming, and transnational fandom. These contexts should guide sampling, not predetermine what every generation remembers.

### 7.3 Cultural memory and intergenerational transmission

Scenes persist through memory, stories, archives, anniversary concerts, covers, family listening, and renewed media exposure (Bennett & Rogers, 2016). This makes generational identity more complex than an age bracket. Younger listeners may inherit older music through parents, older peers, films, games, algorithms, viral clips, or local canonization. Older listeners may adopt new genres. Music cultures therefore contain both formation-period attachment and continuing acquisition.

The author’s prior repository research usefully proposed separating preference content, preference breadth, engagement, and emotional weighting. That model remains a hypothesis-generating device rather than an established finding. Its main contribution to this review is conceptual discipline: what a person likes, how diverse their listening is, how much they listen, and how emotionally important a repertoire feels are different variables.

## 8. Peer Groups, Substance Use, and the Limits of Causal Claims

### 8.1 Representations and exposure

Content analyses show that substances appear in popular music and are often represented positively. Primack et al. (2008) found explicit references to tobacco, alcohol, or other drugs in a substantial portion of sampled popular songs, with large differences by genre. Hall et al. (2013) identified historical changes in substance references across Billboard songs from 1959 to 2009. Hardcastle et al. (2015) similarly examined alcohol portrayals in UK chart music.

These studies establish that listeners may encounter substance-related imagery. They do not establish that exposure causes use. Content frequency is not equivalent to attention, interpretation, identification, memory, or behavior. A lyric can celebrate, condemn, narrate, fictionalize, or ambiguously reference a substance.

### 8.2 Musical preference, peers, and behavior

Mulder et al. (2010) analyzed a nationally representative sample of 7,324 Dutch adolescents and found that perceived peer substance use wholly or partly mediated associations between musical style preferences and smoking, drinking, and cannabis use. The study supports the importance of peers, but its cross-sectional mediation cannot establish temporal causal order. Adolescents may choose friends with similar behaviors and tastes, adopt norms from friends, or share background factors that produce both.

Slater and Henry’s (2013) prospective study provides stronger temporal evidence for a peer-group mediation pathway between music-related media exposure and substance-use initiation. Even prospective mediation, however, cannot eliminate all residual confounding. Media exposure may reflect earlier dispositions, family environment, sensation seeking, or social context.

An observational study also reported an association between exposure to cannabis references in music and adolescent cannabis use (Primack et al., 2010). This is evidence of patterned correlation, not proof that music independently caused use. The most defensible model is reciprocal: prior dispositions shape media selection; music supplies representations; taste helps structure friendship; peers reinforce norms; and behavior may further shape media preference.

### 8.3 Why genre stereotypes fail

Calling hip-hop listeners gangsters or metal listeners dangerous converts group-level representations into individual diagnoses. It also ignores variation within genres and the social distribution of policing, stigma, and visibility. Rock histories contain extensive associations with alcohol and drugs, while pop, dance, country, and rap also represent substances in changing ways. No genre owns substance use.

Genre preference should never be used as a screening instrument for criminality or behavioral risk. Research should directly measure peer behavior, nightlife participation, family monitoring, sensation seeking, socioeconomic position, platform exposure, and local norms. Bangladesh-specific evidence is currently too limited to infer a relationship between genre affiliation and substance use prevalence.

## 9. Music in Protest, War, Violence, and Peacebuilding

### 9.1 Expression, testimony, and counterpublic voice

Music can make private injury publicly intelligible. Lyrics, performance, repetition, and collective listening allow fear, anger, humiliation, hope, and grief to be voiced in forms that can travel beyond their original speaker. In settings where journalism, assembly, or formal representation is restricted, songs may function as counterpublic speech. They can document abuses, ridicule authority, preserve names and events, or provide an emotionally usable vocabulary for grievances that already exist.

Expression is not identical to mobilization. A song can articulate outrage without producing a protest, and people can join a protest without sharing the song’s interpretation. Music becomes politically consequential when it is joined to organizations, networks, performances, broadcasts, platforms, and opportunities for action. It is best understood as part of an infrastructure of expression rather than as a self-sufficient political actor.

Northern Uganda illustrates music’s multiple post-conflict functions. Community-based research identified songs as resources for education, voice, memory, and healing, based on a corpus of conflict-related music and interviews and focus groups with community members and artists (McClain Opiyo, 2015). This case also shows why “political music” should not be reduced to slogans. A song can testify, mourn, teach, and negotiate memory at the same time.

### 9.2 Protest, mass outrage, and collective mobilization

Protest music can condense a complex conflict into repeatable phrases, recognizable symbols, and shared emotional cues. At a march or rally, singing may mark participation, reduce the feeling of isolation, sustain attention, and create a temporary sense of collective capacity. Recordings can also circulate beyond physical gatherings, linking local outrage to diasporic or transnational publics. These are mechanisms of framing, identification, emotional alignment, and communication, not proof that a song caused the mobilization.

Mass outrage is usually produced by a combination of material conditions and interpretive processes. State violence, inequality, repression, scandal, economic hardship, or a triggering event may generate grievance. Political organizations and informal networks identify targets and courses of action. Music can then help name the injustice, narrate who “we” are, preserve momentum, and make participation feel meaningful. The same anthem may be adopted by groups with different goals, while authorities may censor, appropriate, or neutralize it.

Hip-hop is especially visible in this process because it can join testimony, locality, accusation, and rhythmic repetition. Its use in protest does not make hip-hop intrinsically revolutionary, any more than commercial success makes it politically empty. Bangladeshi hip-hop activism belongs within a longer national history in which music and collective movements repeatedly overlap (Hasan & Kundu, 2022).

### 9.3 War, propaganda, and the construction of enemies

War music can recruit, commemorate, intimidate, coordinate, entertain, maintain morale, and distinguish allies from enemies. These functions may be delivered by state institutions, armed organizations, commercial media, soldiers, or civilians. Music’s mnemonic and affective qualities make political categories easier to repeat and embody. It may turn an abstract nation into a felt community, or turn a heterogeneous population into a simplified enemy.

The strongest causal claims require evidence of more than violent lyrics. Researchers must ask who produced and distributed the music, which audiences encountered it, how it was interpreted, whether it was integrated with commands or targeting information, and what coercive or organizational machinery already existed. Research on Radio-Télévision Libre des Mille Collines in Rwanda argues that music and entertainment helped make extremist broadcasting ordinary and socially familiar while genocidal ideology and mobilizing messages circulated through the same medium (McCoy, 2009). The genocide cannot be explained by music or radio alone. The case demonstrates how an everyday sonic environment can amplify organized propaganda when state breakdown, armed organization, racialized ideology, and actionable information are already present.

Informal music within military culture can also perform enemy-making. Sion’s (2026) analysis of soldier-composed Israeli war songs from the First and Second Intifadas traces changing depictions of Palestinians and argues that repeated cultural performance can sediment enmity and normalize extreme violence. This is evidence about symbolic and institutional normalization, not a claim that any song automatically produces violent conduct among listeners.

### 9.4 When music can amplify violence

Music can contribute to escalation when several conditions converge: it dehumanizes a target; is repeated by trusted or authoritative sources; circulates within a closed or polarized network; accompanies threat, humiliation, or perceived emergency; connects identity to violent duty; and appears alongside organizational capacity and opportunity for attack. In that configuration, music may lower moral distance, reinforce group norms, reward aggression, or make violent action seem ordinary and collective.

MacLachlan’s (2022) study of anti-Muslim hate music in Burma/Myanmar examines a corpus of online songs through the legal and analytical concept of incitement. The study treats the songs not as detached artworks but as components of a wider communicative environment in which dehumanizing discourse circulated. Love’s (2016) study of white-power music likewise shows how music can teach racial ideology, recruit young participants, build movement identity, and promote violence. These cases justify concern without licensing genre-wide suspicion. The relevant variable is not whether music is rap, rock, folk, or pop, but how particular texts and performances are embedded in extremist networks and actionable political projects.

The phrase “music turns violence into more violence” should therefore be treated as a conditional hypothesis. Music may intensify arousal, validate revenge, narrate martyrdom, or harden an us-versus-them boundary. It can also provide symbolic release, document suffering, discourage retaliation, or expose the human cost of conflict. Direction depends on content, performance, audience interpretation, institutional sponsorship, repetition, and the surrounding conflict.

| Political function | Musical mechanism | Possible outcome | Causal caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expression and testimony | Narration, witnessing, mourning | Recognition, memory, public voice | Expression does not itself produce collective action |
| Protest mobilization | Framing, repetition, shared performance | Solidarity, visibility, persistence | Grievance, networks, and political opportunity remain necessary |
| Propaganda and recruitment | Simplified narrative, authority, emotional reward | Legitimation, recruitment, morale | Measure exposure, interpretation, organization, and competing messages |
| Enemy construction | Dehumanization, ridicule, boundary marking | Moral exclusion, normalized hostility | Violent representation is not behavioral proof |
| Incitement and coordination | Actionable messages joined to targeting and capacity | Heightened risk of violence | Strong claims require contextual and temporal evidence |
| Peacebuilding | Encounter, cooperation, shared creation, remembrance | Relationship-building, dialogue, healing | Musical contact can reproduce inequality or conceal unresolved conflict |

### 9.5 Memory, healing, and peacebuilding

The same affordances that support mobilization can support de-escalation. Shared performance may create structured contact, allow competing memories to be heard, and establish temporary practices of cooperation. Howell’s (2021) relational framework argues that music-based peacebuilding should be evaluated through the kinds of peaceful relations enacted rather than through the assumption that musical harmony naturally creates social harmony. Her work on post-conflict Sri Lanka similarly concludes that reconciliation effects are possible but must be strategically designed and assessed in context (Howell, 2019).

Music-based peacebuilding can fail. A project may include participants symbolically while leaving material inequality unchanged, privilege elite repertoires, suppress disagreement in the name of harmony, or expose participants to political risk. The relevant question is not whether music is universally peaceful, but which relationships, institutions, and forms of recognition a musical practice creates and whether they persist outside the event.

### 9.6 Bangladesh: from language and liberation to activist rap

Bangladesh offers an unusually clear case of music moving across protest, war, nation-building, memory, and contemporary dissent. Songs of the Language Movement helped preserve linguistic grievance and collective aspiration (Ila, 2017). During the Liberation War, Swadhin Bangla Betar Kendra joined songs, political messaging, information, morale, and counter-propaganda. Historical research treats this broadcasting as a contribution to resistance, not as the sole or primary cause of victory (Hossain et al., 2019). Analysis of the documentary *Muktir Gaan* likewise presents music as a cultural and political resource for solidarity, nationalism, and struggle (Rahman & Rahman, 2023).

Liberation songs later became sites of memory and institutional authority. Their meanings are not frozen in 1971. Performance at state commemorations, cultural events, schools, media programs, and protests can renew national solidarity, but can also invite disagreement about ownership, secularism, exclusion, and the legitimate meaning of the nation. Rabindrasangīt and other heritage repertoires similarly participate in continuing contests over national identity (Bajpaie, 2025).

Contemporary Bangla rap extends this history through a different media ecology. Rap can translate localized grievance into direct address, circulate rapidly through social platforms, and link youth identity to political speech (Hasan & Kundu, 2022). A future empirical study should examine specific protest cycles, including the July 2024 uprising, without assuming that circulation equals persuasion or that politically visible songs represent all participants. It should compare release dates, platform circulation, protest events, audience interpretations, censorship, artist risk, and the role of peer networks. This design would allow claims about expression and amplification while avoiding an unsupported claim that music independently created mass outrage.

## 10. Integrated Model: How Music and Culture Shape Each Other

The synthesis supports a six-stage reciprocal model:

1. **Position and disposition.** Listeners enter musical life with particular family histories, languages, class resources, gender positions, religious contexts, personalities, political experiences, and geographic opportunities.
2. **Selection and encounter.** They encounter music through family, peers, scenes, schools, media, algorithms, venues, and chance events. Existing dispositions influence what receives attention.
3. **Identification.** Particular sounds, lyrics, artists, images, or communities become useful for emotion, self-description, aspiration, memory, or distinction.
4. **Performance.** Musical identification becomes visible through speech, fashion, playlists, attendance, musicianship, dancing, collecting, posting, or political participation.
5. **Social reinforcement and contestation.** Peers and institutions reward, reinterpret, stigmatize, commercialize, or reject those performances.
6. **Cultural reproduction and change.** Repeated practices stabilize a scene or genre culture, while new technologies, generations, politics, and hybridizations alter its meaning.

This model explains why music can be consequential without being mechanically causal. Music supplies resources and settings. People and institutions use them under unequal conditions.

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 Genre cultures are real but internally contested

Rockers, rappers, metal listeners, pop fans, and folk practitioners can inhabit recognizably different cultural worlds. They may prefer different venues, fashions, media, conversational knowledge, forms of bodily participation, and standards of authenticity. These differences warrant research. The error lies in treating them as complete, homogeneous ways of life.

The contrast among Nirvana, Pearl Jam, Audioslave, Dream Theater, and Nile demonstrates variation even inside guitar-centered music. Grunge can foreground alienation and vulnerability; political alternative rock can emphasize dissent; progressive metal can reward mastery and complex listening; death metal can organize symbolic extremity and technical expertise. These repertoires may shape a listener’s vocabulary for interpreting experience, but the listener remains socially situated and capable of selective appropriation.

Hip-hop requires the same differentiation. Gangsta imagery is a legitimate object of analysis when the study asks how crime, policing, masculinity, territory, or authenticity are represented. It becomes prejudice when extended to hip-hop as a whole or to listeners’ conduct without evidence. A credible paper studies the stereotype, its commercial history, and its social effects rather than reproducing it as a finding.

### 11.2 Bangladesh revises Western genre theory

Bangladesh demonstrates that scene, subculture, and authenticity travel imperfectly. The Dhaka metal underground provided alternative space and distinction, yet commercial crossover did not necessarily mean ethical betrayal (Quader & Redden, 2015). Participation depended on middle-class capital and access (Quader, 2016). National identity was simultaneously shaped through heritage forms whose meanings remain contested (Bajpaie, 2025). Digital fandom now produces transnational identities through commercial pop (Sultana & Nibir, 2024).

These cases do not fit a simple opposition between authentic local culture and imported commercial music. Local identity can be produced through global metal, global pop, and hybrid folk instrumentation. Conversely, heritage music can be institutionalized, contested, and politically mobilized. The local is not automatically authentic, and the global is not automatically alienating.

### 11.3 Music’s social effects are conditional

The review supports cultural influence in a conditional sense. Music can normalize vocabularies, make emotions publicly legible, organize friendship opportunities, sustain memory, and provide models of status or resistance. Whether these possibilities become conduct depends on interpretation, repetition, identification, peer reinforcement, institutional context, and available resources.

This conditional model is especially important for substance-use research. The evidence justifies concern about representations and peer-mediated associations. It does not justify genre-based moral panic. Prevention research should focus on media literacy, peer norms, social settings, and direct risk factors rather than stigmatizing taste communities.

## 12. Research Gaps and Proposed Bangladesh Study

### 12.1 Gaps

The evidence reveals seven priority gaps. First, Bangladesh lacks a contemporary, multisite map of musical identity across genres. Second, mainstream pop is less studied than rock, metal, folk fusion, and heritage music. Third, Dhaka dominates the evidence, leaving secondary cities, rural settings, and diasporic communities underrepresented. Fourth, age and generation are discussed more often than they are properly designed. Fifth, platform infrastructures are insufficiently integrated into scene research. Sixth, Bangladesh-specific longitudinal evidence on musical preference, peer networks, and substance use is absent. Seventh, research rarely connects protest-song content and circulation to event timelines, audience interpretation, organizational networks, repression, or later political behavior. This leaves expression, mobilization, and behavioral effect insufficiently distinguished.

### 12.2 Proposed sequential mixed-method design

The following study is a proposal, not research already conducted.

**Phase 1: Multisite qualitative mapping.** Interviews and participant observation should be conducted in Dhaka, at least one secondary city with an active scene, and one less metropolitan setting. Sampling should include rock and metal musicians and listeners, rappers, pop performers and fans, folk or fusion practitioners, K-pop and other transnational fan communities, promoters, venue workers, and listeners who do not strongly identify with a genre. Maximum-variation recruitment should address gender, class, education, religion, language background, age, and occupation.

**Phase 2: Survey and ego-network study.** A survey should measure artist and genre preference, strength of musical identification, functions of listening, local and national identity, peer-network composition, platform use, concert participation, socioeconomic position, family monitoring, sensation seeking, religiosity, and substance use. Measures require Bangla translation, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing, and measurement-invariance analysis. Recruitment should extend beyond universities and music venues.

**Phase 3: Longitudinal network panel.** Three or more waves would allow tests of friendship selection and peer influence. Stochastic actor-oriented models or comparable longitudinal network methods could evaluate whether musical similarity predicts tie formation and whether friends’ tastes or behavior predict subsequent individual change.

**Phase 4: Digital and textual analysis.** With ethical and legal safeguards, public artist materials, lyrics, videos, and platform interactions could be analyzed for identity claims, locality, language, political themes, commerciality, substance references, conflict framing, target construction, calls for action, peacebuilding claims, and genre boundary work. A protest-event sub-study could align song releases and circulation with event timelines, censorship, arrests, organizational messaging, and audience interviews. Content findings should be connected to interviews and event evidence rather than interpreted independently.

**Phase 5: Comparative interpretation.** Results should be compared with mechanism-matched cases from other countries. The purpose would not be to rank cultures, but to identify which mechanisms travel and which depend on Bangladesh’s linguistic, political, religious, and material conditions.

### 12.3 Proposed hypotheses

The quantitative phases could test the following preregistered hypotheses:

- Musical similarity will predict new friendship formation after adjustment for shared school, gender, age, and socioeconomic background.
- Peer behavior will mediate some associations between musical affiliation and substance use, but substantial selection effects will remain.
- Strong scene participation will predict clearer genre-based identity than preference ratings alone.
- Platform-centered fandom will produce more geographically dispersed networks than venue-centered scenes.
- Class resources will predict opportunities for active musicianship and scene participation more strongly than private listening preference.
- Age differences in musical engagement will be partly explained by listening context and cohort, not age alone.
- Political music will predict stronger perceived solidarity among highly identified listeners, but any association with protest participation will be conditioned by prior grievance, peer participation, organizational contact, and perceived political opportunity.
- Exposure to dehumanizing music will not be treated as a sufficient cause of violent behavior; any observed association will be tested against prior hostility, network polarization, authoritative endorsement, and access to organized violence.

These hypotheses should be falsifiable and should not be converted into results before data collection.

## 13. Limitations

This review has several limitations. It was structured and documented but not exhaustive. Subscription databases were not searched comprehensively, independent dual screening was not used, and some sources were available only through abstracts or publisher metadata. English-language indexing likely underrepresents Bangla scholarship. The Bangladesh evidence base is small, uneven across genres, and heavily centered on Dhaka.

Genre categories are unstable. “Rock,” “pop,” “metal,” “rap,” and “dance” do not denote the same sounds or social worlds across countries and periods. Artist examples illuminate possible repertoires but do not establish typical listener beliefs. The review also combines studies with different units of analysis, including lyrics, preferences, scenes, friendships, media exposure, and national memory. Their findings are complementary but not directly commensurable.

Much age research is cross-sectional, leaving age, period, and cohort partially confounded. Much substance-use research is observational, leaving self-selection and residual confounding unresolved. Conflict research introduces additional attribution problems: songs often circulate during rapidly changing events in which repression, organizational strategy, economic conditions, rumor, platform dynamics, and interpersonal networks change simultaneously. The proposed empirical study would improve temporal and contextual evidence but could not fully eliminate causal uncertainty.

Finally, the author’s prior AI-assisted repositories improved source discovery but carried unresolved claims and citation risks. This manuscript includes only independently checked scholarly records and marks proposed interpretations as such.

## 14. Conclusion

Music does not dictate a lifestyle, but it helps make lifestyles imaginable, recognizable, and shareable. Genres provide cultural repertoires through which people interpret emotion, status, morality, place, memory, resistance, aspiration, and belonging. Rock, metal, hip-hop, pop, folk, and electronic music can support different scene practices and philosophies, yet their boundaries overlap and their meanings remain contested.

The strongest account is reciprocal. People select music that fits existing dispositions and circumstances. Music offers symbols, narratives, affects, and communities. Peers, platforms, venues, industries, political organizations, states, and armed groups reinforce or challenge those meanings. Repeated participation can then reshape identity and cultural practice. In moments of conflict, music may transform diffuse feeling into shared expression, strengthen protest identity, normalize an enemy image, carry propaganda, or support remembrance and repair. None of these outcomes follows from sound alone.

Bangladesh demonstrates why geography and history matter. Language movements, liberation memory, folk and syncretic traditions, urban metal, Bangla rock, activist rap, commercial pop, and transnational fandom coexist in a layered musical ecology. Global genres are neither simply imported nor simply resisted. They are localized through Bangla language, class resources, political experience, religious contestation, media infrastructure, and generational memory.

This framework permits serious analysis of gangster imagery in rap, alienation in grunge, political dissent in alternative rock, mastery in progressive metal, extremity in death metal, and intimacy in pop fandom without turning cultural patterns into stereotypes. The resulting question is not “What kind of person does this music create?” It is “Under what social conditions does this music become a resource for becoming, belonging, and acting?”
