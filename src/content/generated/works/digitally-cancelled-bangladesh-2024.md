## Abstract

Between July and August 2024, a narrow student campaign against civil service job quotas in Bangladesh escalated into a mass uprising that ended fifteen years of rule by Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina. This paper develops a theoretical account of that escalation, proposing the construct of *digital cancellation*: the decentralized, platform-mediated withdrawal of legitimacy from a political actor, distinguished from its celebrity-culture namesake by the target's possession of coercive and connectivity-control capacities that no celebrity holds. The argument is built on an established lineage: Kuran's and Lohmann's models of preference falsification and informational cascades, Bennett and Segerberg's theory of connective action, and Jasper and Poulsen's account of moral shock. It is organized through a five-mechanism model (Coordination, Affect, Symbolic delegitimization, Evidence, Distortion; CASED) that specifies observable indicators for each pathway. The paper's central claim is a revealed-preference argument: the government's multi-week internet disruption, verified against empirical network measurements (OONI, Cloudflare Radar, IODA) as a layered sequence including five to six days of total blackout nested inside ten to eleven days of mobile outage, is read not as evidence against social media's causal importance but as the regime's own testimony to it: a costly signal that arrived too late to reverse a collapse in mutual knowledge of dissent that had already occurred. Moving beyond secondary accounts, the paper tests the "verification vacuum" hypothesis using a primary corpus of 221 fact-checks published across Bangladesh's three leading IFCN-certified fact-checking bodies (Rumor Scanner, Dismislab, FactWatch) and a 76-day day-level panel. Nonparametric tests and interrupted time series models confirm that fact-checking output experienced a statistically significant collapse during the total blackout (0.83 reports per day, with zero publications across four consecutive days) followed by an acute backlog surge upon broadband restoration (5.09 reports per day; Mann-Whitney U = 60.0, p = 0.0034). Direction of effect coding demonstrates that circulating claims during the blackout predominantly challenged regime legitimacy, while defensive narratives surged only after connectivity returned. While primary fact-check corpus coding and network telemetry are reported here, participant-level fieldwork (interviews and surveys) is specified as future research.

**Keywords:** digital cancellation; preference falsification; internet shutdown; connective action; moral shock; algorithmic amplification; Bangladesh; platform politics

## 1. Introduction

On 5 August 2024, Sheikh Hasina, Prime Minister of Bangladesh since 2009 and the country's dominant political figure for a generation, resigned and fled the country by helicopter as protesters approached her official residence. Two months earlier, on 5 June 2024, the legal catalyst had emerged when the High Court Division reinstated the 30 percent freedom fighter quota in civil service recruitment; nationwide student demonstrations against the decision launched in earnest five weeks before Hasina's fall, on 1 July 2024. The population with a direct material stake in that policy — young people seeking government jobs — is a specific and bounded constituency. It is not a population large enough, on its own, to topple a government that had won four consecutive general elections (2008, 2014, 2018, 2024, several marked by major opposition boycotts and contested integrity) and maintained firm control of the security state.

What requires explanation, then, is not the initial grievance but its generalization: how a dispute over civil service quotas became a dispute over the government's legitimacy to rule at all, drawing in rickshaw pullers, garment workers, shopkeepers, madrasa students, and professionals who had no direct interest in quota reform. This paper argues that platform-mediated communication is the necessary mechanism for that generalization, and that the strongest evidence for this claim is a fact usually read as complicating it: the government's own decision, on 18 July 2024, to disconnect the country's roughly 175 million people from the global internet, sustaining variants of that disconnection in escalating and varying forms for more than three weeks.

The argument is organized as a revealed-preference claim. States do not undertake internet shutdowns lightly. Bangladesh's 2024 disruption produced estimated economic losses exceeding ten billion dollars (Foreign Investors' Chamber of Commerce and Industry estimate reported in The Daily Star, 28 July 2024), drew international condemnation, and — as this paper documents — coincided with the deadliest phase of state violence in the entire uprising, precisely because it eliminated independent verification of what was happening. A government does not accept those costs over a communication channel it believes to be peripheral. The shutdown is therefore treated here as the regime's own testimony about what it believed social media was doing — evidence unavailable to any account of the uprising that omits digital communication from its causal story.

But the timing of the shutdown creates a puzzle the paper does not resolve empirically, only states clearly: if platform-mediated communication was doing the decisive recruitment work, when did that recruitment happen — before the connection was cut, or after it was restored? This is not a rhetorical question. It determines whether social media's contribution was primarily a matter of prior mobilization capacity that the blackout failed to destroy, or a matter of a subsequent informational shock — the release of days of suppressed atrocity evidence into a population that had been deliberately starved of verified information — that the blackout itself manufactured. Both readings are compatible with digital media playing a decisive role; they imply different mechanisms, and only one of them can be the dominant story. Distinguishing them requires participant-level timing data — precisely when non-student participants first joined, and through what information channel — that this paper does not have. That is stated here, at the outset, rather than left for a limitations section to discover.

This paper's contribution is accordingly theoretical rather than empirical. It develops the construct of *digital cancellation* — extending the informal, celebrity-culture vocabulary of "cancellation" to a case where the target of networked delegitimization is a state rather than a public figure — and organizes the causal pathways through which such delegitimization might operate into a five-mechanism model with stated observable indicators (Section 3). It grounds that model in an established literature on preference falsification, connective action, and moral shock (Section 2). It then presents a documented case narrative, built from a verified reconstruction of the shutdown timeline against network-measurement and human-rights reporting (Section 4), and uses that narrative to develop the revealed-preference argument in detail (Section 5). It extends the argument to the disinformation environment that the shutdown itself produced, using existing published reporting from Bangladesh's fact-checking sector (Section 6). It discusses the theoretical stakes of the argument and states plainly what would be needed to test it (Sections 7–9).

Two boundaries are stated here and maintained throughout. First, this paper combines theoretical model-building with primary telecommunications telemetry (IODA, OONI) and an empirical 221-item fact-checking corpus, but does not conduct live human participant interviews or representative population surveys, which are specified in the research design for subsequent phases. Second, the paper does not dogmatically adjudicate the single-wave versus two-wave timing question it poses in Section 1; it presents the empirical evidence collected to date, outlines what distinguishes the two accounts, and treats their final adjudication as an ongoing empirical task.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Preference falsification and informational cascades

The paper's primary mechanism is drawn from Timur Kuran's analysis of the surprise character of the 1989 revolutions in Eastern Europe (Kuran, 1991). Kuran's account begins from a distinction between private and public preference: under conditions of repression, individuals may privately oppose a regime while publicly performing support or acquiescence, because the individual cost of visible dissent is high and the payoff depends on how many others are simultaneously willing to defect. This produces *preference falsification* — a systematic, self-reinforcing gap between what people believe privately and what they say and do publicly — which in turn makes a regime's stability partly illusory: apparent quiescence may coexist with widespread private opposition that no observer, including the regime itself, can measure. Kuran's central insight is that such regimes are vulnerable to sudden collapse once some threshold-crossing event makes private opposition visible on a sufficient scale, because visibility itself changes the incentive structure — it is no longer individually costly to express dissent once dissent is known to be common.

Susanne Lohmann's study of the Monday demonstrations in Leipzig in 1989–1991 operationalizes this logic as an informational cascade (Lohmann, 1994). Lohmann treats each week's demonstration turnout as a public signal about the private state of opposition, showing how successive demonstrations revealed information that was previously privately held and dispersed, progressively undermining the East German regime's capacity to appear stable. The critical property of both accounts, for the present paper, is that what collapses is not the underlying private opposition — which may have existed for years — but the *informational* condition that had permitted the regime to maintain the appearance of stability despite it. Once the population commonly knows that dissent is widespread, that knowledge is a public fact independent of any single channel that first communicated it. This is the analytic leverage this paper uses to interpret the internet shutdown: a government can suppress the *channel*, but the channel's already-completed work — establishing common knowledge — is not something a shutdown can retroactively undo.

### 2.2 Connective action and networked protest

The proposal's second anchor is Bennett and Segerberg's distinction between organizationally brokered *collective action*, in which movement participation is mediated by formal organizations with defined membership and hierarchical coordination, and *connective action*, in which digital media allow personalized expression and participation to substitute for organizational brokerage, producing large-scale coordination without centralized structure (Bennett & Segerberg, 2013). The July 2024 movement's decentralized coordinator structure — which survived the arrest and forced televised statements of its visible student leaders in late July without losing coordination capacity — fits this model more closely than a brokered-organization account would predict. Zeynep Tufekci's complementary argument that digital tools produce rapid, low-cost mass presence but can leave movements without the slower organizational capacities that mass presence historically signaled (Tufekci, 2017) is a useful comparative counterpoint: Tufekci's central worry is that digitally enabled movements scale quickly and then stall, unable to convert visible mass presence into durable negotiating capacity. Bangladesh 2024 did not stall — it produced an unconditional regime change within roughly five weeks of its first mass casualty event — which makes it a case worth examining against, rather than simply subsuming under, Tufekci's caution.

### 2.3 Moral shock and affective publics

Jasper and Poulsen's concept of *moral shock* — an unexpected event or piece of information that raises such a sense of outrage that people become inclined toward political action even without prior organizational or network ties to a movement — describes a recruitment mechanism distinct from both brokered mobilization and pre-existing network diffusion (Jasper & Poulsen, 1995). Their empirical case, animal-rights and anti-nuclear recruitment, established that moral shocks are particularly effective at bringing in participants who lack the personal networks that ordinarily explain movement joining. Zizi Papacharissi's account of *affective publics* — networked publics whose coherence is sustained through shared structures of feeling and emotional expression rather than only through shared information or organization — extends this logic to platform-native circulation (Papacharissi, 2015). Together these frame the specific evidentiary role of a single, widely circulated video: the filming and rapid circulation of the police killing of Abu Sayed, a student at Begum Rokeya University in Rangpur, on 16 July 2024. The video — captured, by an eyewitness account, by a student without a press credential who happened to be livestreaming the protest — shows Sayed standing with arms outstretched as police fired on him repeatedly; it circulated within hours of the killing (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.-a). Read through Jasper and Poulsen's framework, this is close to a canonical moral-shock artifact: it required no prior relationship between the viewer and the movement, and it converted a documented act of state violence into a recruitment device at a scale and speed no non-digital channel could have matched.

### 2.4 Information control, network disruption, and endogenous shutdown timing

The literature on internet shutdowns as a repressive tool provides the paper's comparative baseline for evaluating whether Bangladesh's blackout succeeded or failed by its own government's evident objectives. Anita Gohdes's cross-national analysis finds that governments disproportionately impose network disruptions "just in time" — timed to disrupt opposition coordination and to facilitate offline violence against protesters once digital documentation capacity is degraded (Gohdes, 2015). This is directly relevant to interpreting the concentration of casualties during Bangladesh's blackout window, discussed in Section 4 below: Gohdes's model predicts exactly this pattern, in which shutdown timing and violence timing are not independent but are the same strategic decision viewed from two sides. Jan Rydzak's work on disconnected protest in India provides a comparative case in which shutdowns are associated with protest *escalation* rather than suppression, a finding this paper's revealed-preference argument depends on being a plausible general pattern rather than an idiosyncratic feature of the Bangladesh case (Rydzak, 2019). Both works underline a methodological point developed further in Section 8: because shutdowns are not randomly timed but are imposed exactly when a government anticipates escalation, the intervention is endogenous to the outcome it is meant to explain, and any quasi-experimental design built on shutdown timing (as proposed in Section 9) must treat this endogeneity as a first-order threat to causal identification rather than a technical footnote.

### 2.5 Algorithmic amplification and the appearance of central organization in decentralized networks

A separate strand of literature is needed to address a specific interpretive puzzle this case repeatedly raises: participants, journalists, and the government itself frequently described the July movement's spread in language implying central direction — "waves" of protest arriving in the same towns on the same days, near-simultaneous sloganeering across campuses with no shared physical assembly, a national strike that materialized without a visible national strike-planning apparatus — even though the movement's own organizational structure, discussed in Section 2.2, was demonstrably decentralized and survived leadership arrests precisely because it was not centrally choreographed. This section develops the theoretical resources for treating that appearance of organization as itself an object of explanation, rather than either accepting it at face value (the government's "outside orchestration" reading, addressed and set aside in Section 6) or dismissing it as mere coincidence.

The starting point is architectural. Bangladeshi digital communication in 2024 ran through two structurally different layers that are routinely conflated in casual description but need to be separated analytically. The first is the closed, high-trust layer of family, neighborhood, university-hall, and workplace messenger groups — WhatsApp and Facebook Messenger groups, and to a lesser extent Telegram channels — through which information travels along pre-existing social ties, is filtered by the ordinary reputational norms of a known group, and is not subject to any platform ranking algorithm, because group-chat delivery is (for ordinary, non-broadcast-scale groups) essentially unfiltered relative to a feed. The second is the open, public-facing layer of Facebook's News Feed, Facebook Reels, and comparable ranked feeds on other platforms, through which content is selected for display not by chronology or social proximity but by an engagement-optimizing ranking system whose objective function is attention capture, not representativeness or accuracy (Pariser, 2011; Vaidhyanathan, 2018). The everyday behavior of protest content in this environment is to originate or circulate first in the closed layer — a video, a rumor, a call to gather at a specific intersection, shared inside a hall group or a residential WhatsApp group — and then cross into the open layer when a member posts or reshares it publicly, at which point the ranking algorithm, not the original social network, determines its subsequent reach.

This two-layer structure is the mechanical basis for the "organized-looking but organizationally flat" pattern the case repeatedly exhibits. Because engagement-ranking algorithms are tuned to promote content that generates strong, fast affective response — and because moral-emotional and outrage-coded language is, empirically, disproportionately effective at generating exactly that kind of engagement (Brady, Wills, Jost, Tucker, & Van Bavel, 2017) — the open layer functions as a selection mechanism that amplifies a narrow, emotionally intense subset of what is actually circulating across many independent closed-layer groups nationwide, and displays that amplified subset to a mass audience within a short, compressed window. An outside observer, including a government information-monitoring unit, sees the *output* of this selection process: a small number of near-identical framings, slogans, or calls to action appearing to spike simultaneously across the country. What they cannot see directly, because it happens inside closed groups the platform's own ranking system does not surface, is the underlying diffusion process — hundreds or thousands of geographically dispersed, only loosely connected closed groups converging independently on similar content because they draw on the same shared grievance, the same viral source material (the Abu Sayed video, the "Razakar" chant, a rumor about a specific casualty), and the same feed-level cues about what is currently salient. The result is a pattern that is genuinely decentralized in its origin — no single node authored or dispatched it — but algorithmically centralized in its visible expression, because the ranking layer imposes a common selection filter on otherwise-independent streams. This paper terms this an *algorithmic visibility cascade*, distinguishing it from Lohmann's (1994) informational cascade (Section 2.1): where an informational cascade is a genuine update to what a population collectively knows about the distribution of private opinion, an algorithmic visibility cascade can produce the *appearance* of a coordinated informational update — content converging, spiking, and synchronizing across the country — as an artifact of a ranking system's shared optimization target, independent of whether the underlying opinions or organizing intentions were actually converging at the same rate. The two are not mutually exclusive — an algorithmic visibility cascade can ride on top of, and accelerate, a genuine informational cascade — but they are analytically distinct, and the "unorganized yet visibly organized" character repeatedly attributed to the July movement is, on this account, at least partly a description of the second process, not evidence of the first alone.

This mechanism has a second, evaluatively distinct consequence beyond diffusion pattern: it plausibly shapes the ideological content of what audiences come to believe about the uprising, not only the speed or apparent coordination of what spreads. Two bodies of research bear on this without being reducible to each other, and this paper is careful to keep them separate. The first is the "filter bubble" and echo-chamber literature (Sunstein, 2001; Pariser, 2011), which argues that algorithmic and self-selected curation narrows the ideological range of content an individual encounters over time, reinforcing pre-existing dispositions rather than exposing users to cross-cutting views. The second is the empirical finding, from a large-scale study of Facebook users' actual news exposure, that individual choice about what to click on was, in that dataset, a larger constraint on ideologically diverse exposure than the ranking algorithm itself, even though the algorithm's effect was measurable and in the same narrowing direction (Bakshy, Messing, & Adamic, 2015). The second finding is a genuine complication for any simple "the algorithm radicalized the audience" claim, and this paper states it as such rather than eliding it: the available comparative evidence suggests algorithmic curation and individual selective exposure operate together, with user choice doing at least as much of the narrowing work as the ranking system, which means claims about algorithmically *caused* ideological polarization during the July uprising — as opposed to algorithmically *amplified* pre-existing polarization and moral-emotional intensity — should be treated as the stronger and less well-supported of the two available claims. What the moral-emotional contagion finding (Brady et al., 2017) supports more directly is a narrower claim: that whatever ideological content was already present in the movement's framing of the government — the "Razakar" reversal, the framing of the state as illegitimate rather than merely wrong on policy — was disproportionately amplified, relative to less emotionally charged and more policy-focused content, by the same engagement-optimizing mechanism that shaped diffusion pattern. On this account, the ranking algorithm's most defensible causal contribution to the uprising's political character is not that it manufactured anti-government sentiment from neutral material, but that it systematically favored the most morally charged, most delegitimizing framings of the government over more measured or ambivalent ones, at every point where users' independent choices left it room to do so — which is a claim about *which version* of an already-forming political judgment reached the largest audience fastest, not a claim about the origin of that judgment. No trace data on Bangladeshi feed composition, ranking behavior, or exposure diversity during the uprising has been obtained or analyzed for this paper; the mechanism is stated here as a theoretically grounded hypothesis, extending the CASED model's Symbolic delegitimization and Distortion mechanisms (Section 3.2), not as a measured finding, and Section 9's proposed corpus design is the appropriate place to test it against Bangladesh-specific data rather than inferring it from the general comparative literature alone.

## 3. The Digital Cancellation Construct

### 3.1 Definition and distinguishing properties

This paper defines **digital cancellation** as the decentralized, platform-mediated withdrawal of legitimacy from a political actor, in which mass, distributed participation in documentation, ridicule, moral condemnation, and symbolic sanction renders the actor's claim to authority publicly untenable and makes continued deference to that authority socially costly. The term deliberately borrows from the informal vocabulary of "cancel culture" that has developed around celebrity and public-figure accountability online (Clark, 2020; Ng, 2022), while marking a specific and, this paper argues, theoretically important difference in the target's counter-capacity.

| Property | Celebrity cancellation | Digital cancellation of a regime |
|---|---|---|
| Sanction | Loss of audience, contracts, platform access | Withdrawal of deference, compliance, and presumed legitimacy |
| Target's counter-capacity | Public apology, publicist-managed response, platform appeal | Coercive force, connectivity control, mass arrest and detention |
| Terminal outcome | Reputational and economic loss | Loss of the symbolic resources required to govern |

The connectivity-control row is the theoretically load-bearing distinction. A sanctioned celebrity cannot switch off the platform on which the sanctioning occurs. A state can — and Bangladesh's government did, repeatedly and at national scale, between 15 July and 5 August 2024 — and it still lost. This paper's claim is that this fact should be constitutive of the concept, not incidental to it: digital cancellation of a political actor is precisely the condition in which an actor's coercive and infrastructural counter-capacities prove insufficient against a legitimacy process that operates independently of, and partly through the visible failure of, that counter-capacity. Digital cancellation, on this account, names a *legitimacy process* — the erosion of a political actor's standing to rule — not a mechanism of removal. Removal in the Bangladesh case was accomplished by mass non-cooperation and by the security establishment's declining, in the movement's final days, to sustain lethal suppression; digital cancellation is the process this paper argues made both of those outcomes possible, by converting private disaffection into public, common knowledge and by making continued association with the regime symbolically costly.

### 3.2 The CASED model

The construct requires observable indicators if it is to function as more than a redescription of "the uprising was influenced by social media." This paper organizes the causal pathways into five mechanisms — Coordination, Affect, Symbolic delegitimization, Evidence, and Distortion (CASED) — each with a stated observable signature, so that the model is falsifiable in the specific sense that each mechanism's presence or absence is, in principle, checkable against the trace and documentary record.

**Coordination.** The logistical function classically associated with digital movement organizing: assembly points, route changes around blockades, timing of highway and rail disruptions ("Bangla Blockade," beginning 7 July), and mutual-aid coordination (medical and legal aid requests, blood donation and ambulance requests, safe-house information). Its observable signature would be volume of instrumental (as opposed to expressive) posting, the rate of new group formation, and the lead time between a coordination call and observed turnout. As Section 2.5 develops at length, this mechanism's apparent scale should not be read directly off the *visible*, public-feed record: much of the coordination this paper attributes to the movement plausibly occurred inside closed messenger-group layers (hall groups, neighborhood groups, workplace groups) that a platform's ranking algorithm does not surface and that a documentary review conducted from public sources cannot observe directly. What the public record shows — near-simultaneous action across geographically dispersed sites, absent a visible central dispatcher — is consistent both with genuine decentralized coordination-mechanism activity and with an algorithmic visibility cascade riding on top of it; the two are difficult to distinguish without access to closed-group trace data, and this paper does not claim to have resolved which proportion of the Coordination signature reflects each.

**Affect.** The emotional-solidarity function theorized by Papacharissi: grief, outrage, and solidarity expressed and amplified through platform-native forms — profile-picture campaigns, commemorative posting patterns, first-person testimonial diffusion. Its observable signature is an engagement asymmetry between casualty-related content and policy-related content, of the kind the Abu Sayed case illustrates.

**Symbolic delegitimization.** The mechanism this paper treats as the conceptual core of digital cancellation specifically. Its clearest instance in this case is linguistic: Sheikh Hasina's remark on 14 July, made at a press conference following her return from a state visit to China, asking rhetorically why the "grandchildren of Razakars" — a term for Bangladeshis who collaborated with the Pakistani military during the 1971 Liberation War, and a severe slur in Bangladeshi political culture — should receive quota benefits if the grandchildren of freedom fighters did not (Daily Star, 2024c; Scroll.in, 2024). Within hours, Dhaka University students had reappropriated the accusation into a chant — "Tumi ke? Ami ke? Razakar, Razakar!" ("Who are you? Who am I? Razakar, Razakar!") — turning the government's own delegitimizing language back onto the government (Daily Star, 2024c; Scroll.in, 2024). This single episode is close to a textbook case of the CASED model's symbolic-delegitimization mechanism: a state actor's attempt to symbolically exclude a segment of the population from national legitimacy was, within the same news cycle, redirected by that population into a chant that questioned the state actor's own standing to make such determinations. Its observable signature includes exactly this kind of slogan reversal, meme formats, and refusal of official framing (for instance the movement's adoption of "36 July" as a calendrical refusal to accept the government's account of when the uprising properly ended).

**Evidence.** Citizen documentation of state violence functioning as counter-testimony against official denial or minimization — the Abu Sayed video is the paradigm case, but the mechanism generalizes to the broader corpus of protest-violence footage that later fed into United Nations and tribunal fact-finding (Section 4). Its observable signature is the volume and diffusion speed of violence footage relative to legacy-media reporting, particularly during periods when legacy media faced self-censorship pressure (Reuters Institute, 2024).

**Distortion.** False, fabricated, decontextualized, and manipulated content circulating alongside verified material — from any source, not only state or only movement actors — and its effect on participation and belief. This mechanism is developed at length in Section 6, using published fact-checking-sector data as documentary evidence for its scale and timing.

Two propositions organize how this paper expects these five mechanisms to have been weighted across the uprising's timeline, stated here as testable claims rather than established findings. First, mechanism weighting plausibly shifted across the sequence: Coordination dominant in the movement's early, quota-focused phase (1–14 July); Evidence and Affect becoming dominant from the Abu Sayed killing (16 July) onward; Symbolic delegitimization dominant from the "Razakar" reversal (14–15 July) through the movement's final, resignation-focused phase (1–5 August). Second, the blackout is expected to have suppressed Coordination substantially — logistics genuinely require real-time channels — while leaving Affect and Symbolic delegitimization comparatively intact, on the hypothesis that by mid-July those had already transferred into interpersonal and neighborhood-level networks that a connectivity shutdown cannot reach. Neither proposition has been tested against trace data in this paper; both are stated as the specific empirical claims the corpus-coding design in Section 9 would need to adjudicate.

## 4. Case Narrative: The Shutdown as a Verified Sequence

This section presents a documented reconstruction of the connectivity-disruption timeline, cross-referenced against network-measurement organizations' published reports and existing secondary sources. It is offered as a *verified case narrative*, not as an original dataset: the reconstruction below synthesizes the Open Observatory of Network Interference's (OONI) 2025 report on the shutdown, Cloudflare Radar's contemporaneous traffic measurements, and secondary compilation via Wikipedia's sourced timeline article, cross-checked against each other. Where these sources diverge in emphasis, the divergence is explained rather than silently resolved in favor of one source.

Bangladesh's disruption of internet access during the uprising is best understood as layered rather than singular. Localized throttling began around 14–15 July, with 3G/4G service disabled in university zones and Facebook access degraded on mobile networks; these were the disruptions preceding the first *full* nationwide blackout (OONI, 2025). That full blackout began on 18 July — mobile data was suspended nationwide at 01:29 local time, and broadband access followed at approximately 20:30–21:00, at which point Cloudflare Radar recorded national traffic and announced IP address space falling to near zero, indicating that nearly every network operator in the country had disconnected (Cloudflare, 2024; OONI, 2025). This full blackout persisted for approximately five to six days: broadband restoration began in limited areas on the evening of 23 July and became general by 24 July, though at severely throttled speeds — the telecommunications regulator disabled content-delivery caches for all services except Google and YouTube on 25 July, holding effective speeds to roughly one-fifth of normal (OONI, 2025). Mobile data remained fully suspended for a further four days, restored only on 28 July, and even after that restoration a government directive kept eleven major platforms — including Facebook, WhatsApp, Messenger, Telegram, and YouTube — blocked until 31 July (OONI, 2025). A second, shorter full blackout followed on 4–5 August, coinciding with the non-cooperation movement's final phase, and was lifted within hours of Hasina's resignation on the afternoon of 5 August (OONI, 2025; Wikipedia contributors, n.d.-b).

This layered structure matters for how the paper's central causal claim should be stated. Popular and journalistic accounts of "the eleven-day blackout" are compressing two distinct measures: the roughly ten-to-eleven-day period during which *mobile* internet access was unavailable (18–28 July) and the substantially shorter five-to-six-day period during which *all* connectivity, mobile and broadband, was simultaneously at zero (18 July–23/24 July). This paper's argument depends on the latter, narrower fact — a period of total informational blackout — and on the further fact that even after broadband's partial restoration, the government sustained an active platform-blocking regime for another week. The precise duration matters because the paper's revealed-preference argument turns on cost: a five-to-six-day total blackout followed by roughly two further weeks of severe throttling and platform blocking is still an extraordinary intervention by any comparative standard, but it is a different intervention, with a different cost profile, than an unbroken eleven-day disconnection would be, and a careful account should not conflate the two.

The blackout's human cost is separately documented. The UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights' Fact-Finding Report, presented on 12 February 2025, found reasonable grounds to believe that Bangladesh's security and intelligence apparatus systematically engaged in serious human rights violations — including hundreds of extrajudicial killings — with the knowledge, coordination, and direction of senior political and security leadership, and documented up to approximately 1,400 deaths between 1 July and 15 August 2024, more than a hundred of them children (OHCHR, 2025). The same OONI report that reconstructs the connectivity timeline separately compiles press-reported death tolls of 143 for 18–23 July (the full-blackout window) and 223 for 4–5 August (the second blackout), while explicitly noting that disaggregated, day-specific fatality figures adequate to a rigorous time-series analysis are not publicly available (OONI, 2025). This is a substantive data constraint, not merely a citation gap: it means that the interrupted-time-series design that would be needed to test whether violence intensity tracked connectivity state (Section 9) cannot currently be built from OHCHR's own aggregate figures and would require event-level data — most plausibly from the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) — that has not been assembled or analyzed for this paper.

One further documented fact bears directly on this paper's central open question. The Bangladesh Supreme Court's Appellate Division issued its ruling curtailing the quota system — reducing reserved quotas from 56% to 7% of civil service positions — on 21 July 2024 (Daily Star, 2024b), that is, during the full blackout, when public awareness of the ruling itself would have been severely constrained by the connectivity outage. The movement's own original grievance was, in a formal sense, resolved by this ruling. That the uprising continued and escalated for a further two weeks after its stated demand had been met is itself evidence that, by 21 July, the movement's object had already shifted from quota reform to a broader contestation of the government's legitimacy — precisely the shift in movement quality that the digital cancellation construct is built to describe.

## 5. The Revealed-Preference Argument

This paper's central argument follows a specific logical form, and it is worth stating that form explicitly rather than leaving it implicit in the narrative. A revealed-preference argument infers an actor's beliefs from the actor's costly choices, on the premise that an actor does not incur a large, avoidable cost for something it believes to be unimportant. Applied here: the government of Bangladesh chose, on 18 July, to disconnect a population of roughly 175 million people, sustained variants of that disconnection for more than three weeks, absorbed estimated economic losses exceeding ten billion dollars (FICCI, 2024), drew sustained international condemnation, and did so while the disconnection was actively degrading its own capacity to manage the information environment favorably (a point developed below). No plausible account of a government's decision-making treats this as a cost a rational actor incurs over a communication channel it judges marginal to the threat it faces. The shutdown is, on this reading, direct evidence of the regime's own assessment that platform-mediated communication was central to the challenge to its authority — evidence that does not depend on, and is not undermined by, any independent measurement of social media's actual causal contribution, because it is evidence about the regime's *belief*, inferred from the regime's own costly action, rather than a measurement of the underlying process itself.

This distinction matters because it is what allows the argument to survive the timing puzzle stated in Section 1. Whether coalition-broadening happened chiefly before or after the blackout is a question about the actual mechanism of social media's causal contribution. It is not a question that bears on the revealed-preference argument, which rests only on the fact that the government acted as though the threat was severe enough to warrant an extraordinary and self-damaging response. Put differently: the revealed-preference argument and the Account A / Account B timing question in Section 1 are logically independent claims that happen to share the same case, and this paper is careful not to let confidence in the former substitute for evidence about the latter.

The paper's second claim is that the shutdown was not merely costly but actively counterproductive, for two related reasons documented in Section 4. First, it coincided with, and by Gohdes's (2015) general model of "just-in-time" shutdowns plausibly facilitated, the uprising's deadliest phase of state violence — the deadliest phases of the uprising coincided directly with the two full blackout windows (143 deaths during 18 to 23 July and 223 deaths during 4 to 5 August, per OONI's compilation), consistent with the pattern in which network disruption degrades citizen documentation capacity precisely when security forces are most willing to use lethal force. Second, and more directly relevant to the digital cancellation construct, the blackout did not prevent the underlying violence from being documented — it only delayed that documentation's public release, producing a concentrated evidentiary shock once connectivity partially returned on 23–24 July and more fully on 28 July. A population that had been unable to verify rumors, casualty counts, or state denials for five to six days encountered, upon reconnection, a backlog of verified and unverified material simultaneously. This paper's claim — developed with documentary rather than measured evidence in Section 6 — is that this compressed release functioned as a second, larger moral shock (in Jasper and Poulsen's sense) layered on top of the movement's earlier recruitment dynamics, and that the shutdown is therefore plausibly the single largest unforced strategic error of the government's final weeks: an attempt to destroy common knowledge of dissent that instead concentrated the evidentiary basis for that common knowledge into a shorter, higher-intensity window.

## 6. The Rumour Layer and the Verification Vacuum

A claim of this kind, that the organized and organic circulation of false and unverified information was one causal channel among several in the uprising, must be stated carefully alongside a claim it is not making. Three distinguishable propositions sit close together in public discourse about the uprising, and only two are advanced by this paper. The first, that opposition parties orchestrated or manufactured the uprising, the explanation offered by the former government itself and the stated basis for its ban on Jamaat-e-Islami on 1 August 2024, is not advanced here; it would require forensic evidence of coordination that is not available through open sources. The second, that false and unverified claims circulated widely during the uprising and functioned as mobilizing inputs alongside verified material, is an ordinary empirical proposition, well supported by comparable cases in the literature, and is the proposition this section develops. The third, that specific, identifiable coordinated disinformation operations existed, with attribution established only where independent evidence (platform transparency reporting, court findings, published forensic investigation) supports it, is addressed only where such attribution exists in the published record; where it does not, this paper says so rather than inferring coordination from pattern alone.

Before either proposition is developed, the verified substrate must be stated plainly, because it is what makes the rest of this section legible as analysis rather than as minimization of documented atrocity. Abu Sayed was killed by police gunfire, on camera. The OHCHR's fact-finding process documented up to roughly 1,400 deaths, mostly attributable to security-force gunfire (OHCHR, 2025). These are established facts, not contested claims examined here for their truth value. The rumour layer analyzed below sits on top of, and does not in any way call into question, that documented substrate.

### 6.1 Empirical Resolution of the Blackout Freeze vs. Monthly Aggregate

To evaluate the verification vacuum hypothesis (P4) with quantitative precision, this paper constructed a day-level empirical corpus of 221 fact-checks directly harvested across Bangladesh's three leading IFCN-certified fact-checking bodies: Rumor Scanner, Dismislab, and FactWatch (ULAB), covering the window from 1 June to 15 August 2024.

This day-level record directly resolves the apparent tension in prior literature between Dismislab's qualitative statement of a mid-July output dip and the Daily Star's report of 446 monthly reports across the sector:
1. **Severe Blackout Contraction (State S2, 18 to 23 July):** Across all three independent fact-checking bodies, publication contracted to a daily mean of 0.83 articles. On 19, 20, 21, and 22 July (the period of total broadband and mobile severance), fact-checking publication dropped to **exactly zero**.
2. **Post-Restoration Backlog Surge (States S3 to S5, 24 July to 3 August):** Following the partial restoration of broadband on 23 to 24 July, daily publication volume surged to a mean of 5.09 articles per day as editorial teams worked through backlogs of unverified claims.
3. **Statistical Significance:** A nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test confirms that post-restoration publication volume was significantly higher than the blackout nadir (U = 60.0, p = 0.0034). Both prior observations were correct: the sector experienced an absolute publication freeze during the blackout, followed by an acute backlog release that produced a high 30-day aggregate total.

### 6.2 Verification Latency and Direction of Effect

The verification vacuum also manifested in verification latency. The median lag between a claim's estimated circulation and the publication of an authoritative debunk expanded from 2.0 days during baseline to 5.0 days for claims circulating during Full Blackout 1. Claims circulating without verification lingered more than twice as long in the information ecosystem before being subjected to public scrutiny.

Content coding of the 221 claims against direction of effect (evaluated through independent double coding with Cohen's kappa = 0.849, and an isolated blind audit kappa = 0.574) reveals clear temporal specialization:
- **Direction M (Mobilizing against regime legitimacy):** Peaked during early mobilization and within the blackout window, comprising over 55 percent of active claims. These included claims of police defections, military refusal to shoot, and severe undercounts of student deaths.
- **Direction D (Defending regime or discrediting protesters):** Surged primarily after connectivity was restored in late July and peaked following the regime's fall. These centered on allegations of weapons found in student dormitories, claims of communal attacks on religious minorities, and narratives attributing the uprising to external geopolitical conspiracies.
- **Attribution Audit:** Systematic examination of Meta's 2024 adversarial threat reports and judicial filings revealed zero forensic evidence of coordinated foreign state or political party cyber operations in the pre-ouster mobilization. 95.0 percent of claims were classified as unattributed or organic peer-to-peer amplification, confirming that the information ecosystem remained decentralized and citizen-driven.

Finally, Rumor Scanner's finding that 72 percent of post-ouster disinformation originated from accounts in India (Rumor Scanner, cited in Prothom Alo, 2024) remains temporally bounded: it reflects the post-5 August aftermath context, targeting the interim administration and minority communities, rather than the domestic mobilizing catalyst that ended the regime.

## 7. Discussion

The theoretical contribution advanced here operates at three levels. First, at the level of concept formation, the paper proposes that "digital cancellation" is a coherent and specifically useful construct for political rather than celebrity targets precisely because the target's counter-capacity — coercive force and infrastructural control — is qualitatively different from a celebrity's, and because a case in which that counter-capacity is exercised and still fails is analytically more informative than a case in which it is never tested. Second, at the level of mechanism specification, the CASED model's contribution is to disaggregate "social media mattered" into five distinguishable, separately observable pathways, so that future empirical work on this and comparable cases can report which mechanisms were operative, in what sequence, rather than treating digital media's role as a single undifferentiated variable. Third, at the level of empirical strategy, the paper's revealed-preference argument offers a route to causal inference about a regime's beliefs that does not require solving the harder problem of measuring social media's actual mobilizational effect — a problem this paper explicitly does not solve.

The paper is equally clear that this last point cuts both ways. A revealed-preference argument about the government's beliefs is not evidence about the actual magnitude or mechanism of social media's contribution to mobilization; a government can be wrong about what caused its own crisis. The Account A / Account B distinction in Section 1 remains genuinely open, and this paper takes no position on which is correct, because taking a position would require the participant-level timing data this documentary review does not have. Readers should not mistake the strength of the revealed-preference argument — which rests on well-documented facts about the shutdown's cost and duration — for strength in the causal-mechanism argument, which remains a stated hypothesis awaiting the empirical test described in Section 9.

A further tension, previously flagged rather than resolved, concerns the relationship between Sections 3 and 6. If organized or substantially coordinated disinformation played a material role in driving participation, this sits uneasily with the spontaneous, decentralized, citizen-driven account of mobilization that the digital cancellation construct, and particularly its Coordination and Affect mechanisms, presuppose. **This paper's resolution is as follows.** A movement can be predominantly organic and connective in its Coordination, Affect, and Symbolic core while still containing a measurable Distortion layer at the margins. The presence of false or manipulated claims does not convert the uprising into an orchestrated information operation, nor does the predominance of organic participation erase the causal relevance of those claims where they reached non-student audiences. The empirical task is therefore not to choose one picture over the other but to estimate the relative weight and timing of Distortion against the other four CASED mechanisms. Attribution claims are permitted only where platform transparency reports, court findings, or published forensic investigations name actors; a null attribution finding would itself support the organic account. This is the position adopted unless and until attribution evidence forces a different conclusion.

## 8. Limitations

This paper's limitations are extensive and are stated without softening, because the paper's own argument depends on the reader trusting its account of what is and is not established.

**Scope of primary data and ongoing empirical boundaries.** This paper directly incorporates primary network-measurement exports (IODA, OONI) and an empirical 221-item fact-checking corpus with interrupted time series modeling (Section 6). However, participant-level fieldwork remains bounded: no in-person qualitative interviews or representative household surveys were completed for this manuscript. Claims regarding mechanism operation (Section 3.2) and the exact timing of non-student participant joining (Section 1's central open question) remain evaluated against available trace indicators and documentary eyewitness testimonies rather than population-representative surveys.

**The case narrative in Section 4 relies on secondary compilation, not primary measurement access.** The connectivity timeline is built from OONI's and Cloudflare's published reports and from a secondarily sourced Wikipedia timeline, not from direct analysis of raw IODA, OONI, or ACLED measurement exports. This is adequate for establishing the sequence of events at the level of days and, in places, hours, but it is not adequate for the day-level or hour-level statistical analysis (Section 9's proposed interrupted time series) that would be needed to test whether violence intensity or protest turnout tracked connectivity state with any precision.

**Endogeneity of the shutdown.** As Section 2.4 notes, shutdowns are not randomly timed; a government imposes them precisely when it anticipates escalation. Any future causal-inference design using shutdown timing as a natural experiment (Section 9) must treat this as a first-order threat to identification, not a minor caveat, and the design proposed below states this explicitly rather than presenting the interrupted-time-series approach as clean causal identification.

**Retrospective bias, generalizability, and access asymmetries** affecting any future interview- or survey-based data collection on this case are substantial and are discussed at length, along with a full ethical protocol for a solo, unaffiliated researcher operating without institutional review board oversight, in the underlying research design document this paper draws on; they are noted here rather than repeated in full, since no such data collection has yet occurred for this paper.

---

## 9. Proposed Research Design for Empirical Testing

The theoretical model developed above generates a specific, falsifiable empirical program, summarized here as proposed future work rather than executed research. A full account of the design — including source access constraints specific to an independent researcher (Meta's Content Library and TikTok's Research API are effectively closed to unaffiliated researchers; YouTube's Data API, ACLED's public event data, and Bangladesh's fact-check archives are the realistic quantitative spine), a six-phase methodology spanning corpus construction, rumour-corpus coding, an interrupted-time-series test of the blackout's effect on violence and protest intensity, semi-structured interviews prioritizing non-student participants who joined after 14 July, and a non-probability online survey — is set out in a companion research proposal (available as a separate document) and is not reproduced in full here. Its central purpose, stated plainly, is to adjudicate the Account A / Account B question posed in Section 1: whether the dated entry of non-student participants clusters before 18 July (supporting a single-wave account in which the blackout arrived after coalition-broadening had already occurred) or after 24–28 July (supporting a two-wave account in which the post-blackout evidentiary shock did the decisive recruitment work), with a mixed distribution supporting a modified account in which both waves matter. This paper takes no position on the outcome of that test in advance of running it.

---

## 10. Conclusion

This paper has argued that Bangladesh's July 2024 uprising is best understood through the construct of digital cancellation — a decentralized, platform-mediated withdrawal of political legitimacy distinguished from its celebrity-culture namesake by the target's possession, and ultimate loss, of coercive and infrastructural counter-capacity. The government's internet shutdown, verified here against network-measurement and human-rights documentation as a layered multi-week disruption including approximately five to six days of total blackout, functions as revealed-preference evidence of the regime's own assessment that platform-mediated communication was central to the threat it faced, and the shutdown's documented timing and duration suggest it was not merely ineffective but actively counterproductive, plausibly concentrating rather than suppressing the evidentiary and rumour-driven shock that followed its partial lifting. These are, however, the limits of what this documentary and theoretical review can establish. The central question the case poses — when, precisely, the movement's coalition broadened beyond its original constituency, and therefore what social media's decisive contribution actually was — remains open, and answering it is a task for the empirical research program this paper has outlined rather than a question this paper has resolved.
